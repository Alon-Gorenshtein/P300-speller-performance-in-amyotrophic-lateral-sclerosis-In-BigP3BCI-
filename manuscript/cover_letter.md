Re: Submission of "Can calibration EEG predict online P300-speller performance in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis? A leave-one-study-out validation study" as a Research Article

Dear Editors of *Journal of Neural Engineering*,

We submit "Can calibration EEG predict online P300-speller performance in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis? A leave-one-study-out validation study" for consideration as a Research Article. P300 spellers remain an important assistive-communication technology for people with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), but online selection performance can vary across users and sessions. Calibration recordings are routinely collected before use, yet whether a calibration-derived EEG measure predicts online performance outside the source cohort in which it was fit has remained unclear. We addressed this question using the public BigP3BCI archive and a leave-one-study-out validation design.

The analysis included 29 ALS study-scoped records, 57 sessions, and 2,537 eligible feedback-phase character selections from three historical source studies. A calibration-only multichannel P300 discriminability score achieved held-out-study AUCs of 0.860, 0.803, and 0.799, with a pooled out-of-study AUC of 0.829 (95% CI, 0.757-0.872). A prespecified Pz amplitude comparator had a pooled AUC of 0.480 (95% CI, 0.370-0.592). Adding recorded ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised values did not improve pooled discrimination. We made the distinction between study-scoped records and unique persons explicit, used only Train-phase data to derive predictors, reconstructed each feedback outcome from source event traces, and report a within-study leave-one-participant-out sensitivity analysis.

We believe the paper is relevant to *Journal of Neural Engineering* for three reasons. First, it tests a clinically meaningful question using the data that would be available before an online BCI session, rather than reusing online labels to create an optimistic predictor. Second, the leave-one-study-out design evaluates transport across related but heterogeneous legacy P300-speller protocols. Third, the findings suggest a practical prospective research question: whether a calibration score can support clinician and user review before a communication session, without restricting access on the basis of an automated prediction. The study does not claim a diagnostic, prognostic, causal, or contemporary clinical-deployment effect.

We thank the editors for their consideration. This work has not been submitted elsewhere and is not under consideration by another journal. We can provide the archive provenance record, analysis code, derived outputs, and source-event reconstruction details to support editorial assessment.
